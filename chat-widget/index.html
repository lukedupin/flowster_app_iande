How Much Commission Do Life Insurance Agents Make? (2026)

Written by Steve Gibbs, JD, AEP®

Estate Planning Attorney · Co-Founder of I&E

Updated September 27, 2026·18 min read

Originally published September 26, 2026

Fact-checked byBarry Brooksby— licensed insurance expert

By Steve Gibbs, JD | Reviewed by Barry Brooksby, Authorized Infinite Banking Practitioner

📖 Estimated Reading Time: 12 minutes

A life insurance agent generally earns 50% to 100% or more of the first year premium, plus smaller renewal commissions for several years after that. What that percentage gets applied to depends on the product. On term insurance it's applied to the full premium you pay. On whole life it's applied to the base premium, and on indexed universal life to the target premium, which means money routed into a paid-up additions rider or paid above target contributes far less to the agent's check.

That distinction is where most published commission figures go wrong, and on a policy built for cash value it's the difference between a number that's roughly right and one that's off by a factor of five.

Two policies with the same $20,000 annual premium, from the same carrier, on the same insured person, can pay one agent about $17,000 and another a few thousand. The buyer of the first ends year one with zero cash value. The buyer of the second ends year one with $18,147.

Same company. Same money. The only variable is how the policy was built.

TL;DR: The Bottom Line

  • First year commission commonly runs 50% to 100% or more, with renewals in years two through ten typically in the 2% to 5% range. Schedules vary by carrier and product.
  • The rate is applied to a defined slice of what you pay rather than to all of it. Paid-up additions, a term rider, and premium above target generally pay the agent a small fraction of the headline rate.
  • Commission figures quoted elsewhere often describe the full distribution payout rather than the writing agent's check. Overrides to the agency and the IMO come out of the same number.
  • Term carries a high percentage on a small premium. Permanent insurance carries a lower percentage on a much larger one, and within permanent insurance the design decides most of the outcome.
  • On the same $20,000 premium, four different designs produce commissions ranging from about $17,000 down to a few thousand, with first year cash value running from $0 to $18,147.
  • In the video below, Barry walks through four of his own designs and points to the one that pays him the least as the one he would want you to own.
  • A high commission does not mean the policy is wrong. It tells you what the policy was built to do. A design built for protection pays more. A design built for accumulation pays less.

The Short Answer

Most life insurance commission is paid once, in the first year, as a percentage of a specific slice of your premium rather than all of it.

That slice has a name. On whole life it's the base premium. On universal life and indexed universal life it's the target premium. Money you pay above that slice, into a paid-up additions rider or as excess premium, is still your money going into the policy. It just tends to pay the agent a small fraction of the headline rate.

This is where most published commission figures go wrong. They quote a percentage and apply it to everything you pay, which produces a number that can be off by a factor of five or more on a policy built for cash value.

Key Point: The percentage tells you very little on its own. 100% of a $600 term premium is $600. 50% of the base premium on a $20,000 whole life policy could be $10,000 or it could be $1,000, depending entirely on how much of that $20,000 was routed to base. The dollar amount is the number worth watching, and the dollar amount is a function of design.

How Life Insurance Agents Actually Get Paid

First Year Commission

The bulk of an agent's compensation on a life policy arrives in year one. The rate is set by the carrier and published in a compensation schedule, and it's applied to a defined portion of premium rather than to the total.

On a whole life policy, that portion is the base premium. A $20,000 annual premium might be structured with $20,000 in base and nothing else, or with $2,000 in base and $17,000 routed to a paid-up additions rider with the remainder in a term rider. Same check from you. Radically different commissionable amount.

On universal life and IUL, the defined portion is called target premium. It's roughly the premium the carrier expects would fund the death benefit to maturity, and it's calculated per policy based on face amount, age, and rating. Premium paid above target is excess premium, and it typically carries a much lower rate. For a closer look at how these numbers appear on paper, see our guide to reading a whole life illustration.

Renewals

Many carriers pay a renewal commission in years two through ten, commonly in the 2% to 5% range, though the schedule varies by carrier and product. That percentage is applied to the premium you contribute in a given year, not to your accumulated cash value. Some pay a service or persistency fee beyond year ten. Renewals are small relative to first year, which is why most of the incentive in this business sits in the first twelve months.

Advances and Chargebacks

Carriers will often advance a portion of expected first year commission at issue rather than paying it out monthly as premium comes in. Six to twelve months is typical. If the policy lapses inside that window, the agent owes money back. Depending on the carrier and how early the lapse happens, that can be the unearned portion or the entire first year commission.

This shapes behavior more than most buyers realize. An advance is a loan against a policy staying in force, so an agent carrying advances has a direct financial stake in placing business that persists. It also means products with heavy advancing get sold differently. Final expense, for example, is commonly advanced nine to twelve months on a full-premium commission, which is part of why that market runs on high-volume phone sales.

Key Takeaway — How the Money Moves

Commission is front-loaded by design, paid on a defined slice of premium, and often advanced against future payments. Those three facts, taken together, explain most of what people find confusing about how agents behave.

Contract Level and the Override Stack

Here's the piece that reconciles the wildly different commission figures published elsewhere.

An agent's contract level is the percentage the carrier has assigned to that agent's code. Street level is the baseline an independent agent typically starts at. Above street, contract levels rise with production and with position in a distribution hierarchy.

Above the writing agent sits an override stack. The agency, the general agent, and the IMO each hold a higher contract level and receive the difference between their level and the level below them. So a single policy can generate compensation at several points in a chain, and the total paid out by the carrier is larger than what the agent who sat across from you receives.

This is why one source says life agents make 50% and another says 120%, and both can be describing the same product. The low figure is usually the writing agent at street level. The high figure is usually the entire distribution chain added together.

Key Point: When you read that an agent "makes 100% of the first year premium," ask which premium and which agent. It's frequently the whole chain, measured against base or target premium rather than against what you actually pay.

Term Life Insurance Commission

Term insurance carries the highest headline percentage in the business and produces the smallest checks.

First year rates commonly land between 50% and 80% at street level, and total distribution compensation can exceed 100% once overrides are counted. Renewals are small, often 2% to 5% in years two through ten, and many term products pay little or nothing after that.

Then the arithmetic. A healthy 35-year-old buying $1,000,000 of 20-year term might pay around $600 a year. At 80%, that's $480. At 120% across the entire chain, it's $720, split among several parties.

Which is the whole story of how term is sold. The economics only work at volume, which is why term is dominated by call centers, online quoting, and rapid-issue underwriting, and why a term sale rarely comes with an hour of design conversation. There isn't an hour's worth of compensation in it.

None of that makes term a bad product. For a household that needs a large death benefit during the years a mortgage and young children are in the picture, term is frequently the right answer, and an honest agent will say so. It does explain why an agent who only sells term has to sell a great many policies, and why an agent looking at a $600 case and a $20,000 case will feel the difference. Our whole life versus term comparison covers when each one fits.

Key Takeaway — Percentage vs. Dollars

Percentage and dollars move in opposite directions across products. Term pays the highest rate on the smallest premium. Permanent insurance pays a lower rate on a much larger premium, and within permanent insurance, the design decides almost everything.

This is where the percentage stops being useful and the dollars start telling you something.

Reading this table: the base and PUA figures are approximate, since the two blended designs also carry term rider premium inside the non-PUA portion. Paid-up additions purchase paid-up insurance, so cash value and death benefit rise together, and the term rider carries additional coverage while the PUA dollars compound underneath it.

The 90/10 design is funded right up to the MEC limit without crossing it, which is what preserves the policy's tax treatment.

When You Get Your Money Back

Year one cash value is the number people fixate on. Break-even is the one that changes what you can actually do with the policy.

The 80/20 design crosses break-even in year six. By then $120,000 has gone in and roughly $120,000 of cash value is sitting there, available. The 90/10 design gets there in under five years, and depending on the insured's age and health it can happen in three or four.

The base-only design, by contrast, is still well behind cumulative premium at year six, because the first year contributed nothing and the second contributed about $3,200 against $40,000 paid in.

That gap is the practical difference between the designs. It is the difference between capital you can reach in a few years and capital that stays out of reach for a decade or more.

The Part That Keeps This Fair

The base-only design is not a scam, and Barry says so on camera. A $20,000 premium buying $1.1 million of death benefit from day one is a reasonable policy to own if legacy is the goal, if the point is a tax-free transfer to the next generation rather than cash you can reach. It's simply a different product built for a different purpose, and the commission is the honest byproduct of a design that routes nearly every dollar into insurance.

What the commission tells you is what the policy was built to do. It doesn't tell you whether the policy is right or wrong. Those are different questions, and most of the internet collapses them into one.

Key Takeaway — What Design Does

Four designs, one carrier, one premium, one insured. The commission ranges from about $17,000 down to a few thousand, and first year cash value runs from $0 to $18,147. Ask what the design is for before you ask what it pays. Our guide to properly structured whole life design covers how these builds are put together.

Five Questions to Ask Before You Sign

You do not need to become an expert in policy design to protect yourself. You need five questions and a willingness to ask them out loud.

  1. What is my first year cash value, and what percentage of premium is that?
    In an accumulation design, first year efficiency above 70% is strong. Below 50% on a policy you were told is for cash value is worth a conversation.
  2. How is the premium split between base and paid-up additions?
    You are entitled to this number. If it isn't on the illustration, ask for it in writing.
  3. Is there a term rider in this design, and what is it doing?
    Term riders let more premium flow to paid-up additions without crossing the MEC limit. Their absence in a cash-value design is a signal.
  4. Can you show me this same premium designed for cash value, side by side?
    One illustration tells you nothing. Two tell you everything. An agent who can run one can run four.
  5. Which of these designs pays you more, and why did you recommend this one?
    The answer may be entirely reasonable. What matters is whether you get a straight one.
Key Point: You are not being rude by asking question five. A policy funded at $20,000 a year is a $400,000 commitment over twenty years. Anyone asking you to make it should be able to discuss their own compensation without discomfort.

IUL Commission and Target Premium

Indexed universal life runs on the same structural logic as whole life, with different vocabulary.

Commission is calculated off target premium rather than base premium. Target is roughly what the carrier expects would fund the death benefit to maturity, and it's set per policy based on face amount, age, and underwriting class. Premium above target is excess premium, and it typically carries a sharply reduced rate.

Which produces the same outcome as whole life by a different route. An IUL funded at minimum death benefit for a given premium, pushed hard toward cash accumulation, generates a small target and therefore a small commission. The same total outlay structured with a larger death benefit generates a much larger target and a much larger commission. See our guide to overfunded life insurance for how these designs are built.

Rates on IUL target premium tend to run higher as a percentage than whole life base commission, though this depends on the carrier and the contract. That difference is largely structural. Many IUL carriers distribute through independent marketing organizations and compete for distribution with first year cash, while mutual carriers writing whole life tend to spread more compensation across renewals and persistency.

So a higher headline percentage can still produce a smaller check. Which is the same lesson as the whole life table, in a different product.

Key Takeaway — Target Premium

On an IUL, ask what the target premium is and what portion of your outlay sits above it. That single number tells you more about the design than the illustrated values do.

Annuity Commission

Annuity compensation works differently enough to be worth its own note.

It's generally a one-time payment calculated on the deposit, commonly somewhere between 1% and 8% depending on the product type and surrender period. Multi-year guaranteed annuities sit at the low end. Fixed indexed annuities with longer surrender schedules sit higher. Most carry no renewal commission, though some products offer a trail option in exchange for a lower upfront payment.

The part buyers most often misunderstand: the commission is not deducted from your deposit. Put $100,000 into an annuity and $100,000 goes to work on day one. The carrier pays the agent from its own general account and recovers the cost through the spread and the surrender schedule.

Which is why the surrender period is the number to examine. Products paying larger commissions generally carry longer surrender schedules, because the carrier needs more time to recover the acquisition cost. The commission itself matters less to you than the liquidity terms it implies. The same is true of what you're buying. A multi-year guaranteed annuity with a stated rate for a set term, or a contract with a guaranteed lifetime income rider, is being bought for the guarantee. What the agent earns placing it tells you very little about whether that guarantee is worth having.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the commission come out of my cash value?

Not directly. Commission is paid by the carrier out of its own funds, not subtracted from your premium as a line item. The effect shows up indirectly, because a design routing most of your premium to base premium produces both a larger commission and lower early cash value. The commission and the low cash value share a cause. One doesn't fund the other.

Do life insurance agents make more on whole life than on term?

In dollars, generally yes, because the premiums are far larger. As a percentage, term often pays more. An agent earning 80% on a $600 term premium receives $480. The same agent on a $20,000 whole life policy receives an amount that depends almost entirely on how the policy is designed.

Why did my policy have no cash value in year one?

Most likely because the premium was routed to base premium rather than to a paid-up additions rider. First year cash value in a base-heavy design can be zero or near zero. That's expected behavior for a design built around death benefit, and a problem only if you bought it believing it was built for cash.

Can I ask my agent what he's paid?

Yes, and you should. Most states don't require proactive disclosure on life insurance, but nothing prevents you from asking, and nothing prevents an agent from answering. How the question is received tells you a good deal on its own.

Do I pay more by using an agent?

No. Life insurance premiums are filed with state insurance departments and are the same whether you buy through an agent, a call center, or directly. Commission is built into the product pricing. Going without an agent doesn't lower your premium. It just removes the design conversation.

Do agents earn more with some carriers than others?

Yes. Compensation schedules vary by carrier, and an agent's contract level varies by carrier as well. The variation between competitive mutual carriers tends to be modest relative to what policy design does to the same premium. Design moved the commission by a factor of five or more in the table above, inside a single carrier's schedule. Carrier differences don't approach that.
